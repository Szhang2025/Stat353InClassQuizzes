§1

Probability

The math of "how sure are we" — sample spaces, independence, and Bayes' theorem.

Work Order #4471Line 4 · SMT Inspection

Scenario. Your PCB assembly line historically ships boards with a 3% true defect rate. An automated optical inspection (AOI) camera flags boards as "defective" — it correctly flags 98% of truly defective boards (its sensitivity), but it also false-flags 5% of good boards (its false positive rate).

Your shift supervisor pulls you aside with three questions before the line restarts:

  1. What fraction of all boards do we expect to get flagged, whether truly defective or not?
  2. If a board gets flagged, what's the actual probability it's defective? (This decides whether flagged boards go to scrap or a second inspection.)
  3. Two independent AOI stations both check high-risk boards — what's the chance both catch a real defect?
Known values P(Defective) = 0.03 → P(Good) = 0.97 P(Flag | Defective) = 0.98 (sensitivity) P(Flag | Good) = 0.05 (false positive rate)

Core ideas

Sample space & events
The sample space S is every possible outcome (e.g., {defective, not defective}). An event is a subset of S you care about, like "flagged."
Definition of probability
A number between 0 and 1 that measures how likely an event is: 0 means it never happens, 1 means it always happens. Formally, \(P(A)\) satisfies three axioms — \(P(A) \geq 0\) for every event, \(P(S) = 1\) for the full sample space, and probabilities of mutually exclusive events add together.
Addition rule
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B). For mutually exclusive events, P(A ∩ B) = 0.
Independence & multiplication rule
A and B are independent if P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B) — knowing one tells you nothing about the other.
Conditional probability
P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) — the probability of A, given that B already happened.
Law of total probability
If a set of events \(A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n\) partitions the sample space (they're mutually exclusive and cover every outcome), the probability of any event B can be built up piece by piece from how likely B is under each \(A_i\), weighted by how likely each \(A_i\) is — exactly how Step 1 of the worked example finds the overall flag rate below.
Bayes' theorem
Flips a conditional around: it turns "P(test positive | actually defective)" — which the sensor spec gives you — into "P(actually defective | test positive)" — which is what you actually need to decide.
Law of total probability\(\displaystyle P(B) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(B \mid A_i)\cdot P(A_i)\)
Bayes' theorem\(\displaystyle P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\cdot P(A)}{P(B)}\)

Worked example

Bench notes — resolving Work Order #4471
Using the known values from the work order:
Step 1 — total flag rate (law of total probability):
\(P(\text{Flag}) = P(\text{Flag}\mid\text{Def})\cdot P(\text{Def}) + P(\text{Flag}\mid\text{Good})\cdot P(\text{Good})\)
\(P(\text{Flag}) = (0.98)(0.03) + (0.05)(0.97) = 0.0294 + 0.0485 = 0.0779\)
Step 2 — Bayes' theorem for the real question:
\(\displaystyle P(\text{Def}\mid\text{Flag}) = \frac{P(\text{Flag}\mid\text{Def})\cdot P(\text{Def})}{P(\text{Flag})} = \frac{0.0294}{0.0779} \approx 0.377\)
Step 3 — two independent stations, both catch a real defect:
\(P(\text{both flag}\mid\text{Defective}) = 0.98 \times 0.98 = 0.9604\)
Answers → ~7.8% of all boards get flagged; only ~37.7% of flagged boards are truly defective (so a second check before scrapping is worth it); two stations together catch ~96% of real defects.

Exercises

Exercise 1.1

Two independent crimping machines feed the same harness assembly. Machine A produces a bad crimp 2% of the time; Machine B, 1.5% of the time. A harness needs both a good crimp from A and from B to pass. What's the probability a harness fails at least one crimp?

P(fail) = 1 − P(both good) = 1 − (0.98)(0.985) = 1 − 0.9653 = 0.0347 (≈3.5%).
Exercise 1.2

A shipment is inspected for two independent defect types: dimensional (occurs 4% of the time) and surface finish (occurs 6% of the time). What's the probability a unit has at least one of the two defects?

P(A∪B) = P(A)+P(B)−P(A)P(B) = 0.04 + 0.06 − (0.04)(0.06) = 0.10 − 0.0024 = 0.0976 (≈9.8%).
Exercise 1.3

Of 400 inspected units logged by shift: Day shift produced 250 units with 10 defects; Night shift produced 150 units with 12 defects. A defective unit is picked at random from the log — what's the probability it came from Night shift?

Total defects = 22. P(Night | Defective) = 12/22 ≈ 0.545 (≈54.5%), even though Night shift makes fewer units overall.
Exercise 1.4

A gas leak sensor has a 99% true-positive rate and a 3% false-positive rate. Only 0.5% of readings correspond to an actual leak. If the sensor alarms, what's the probability there's a real leak?

P(Alarm) = (0.99)(0.005) + (0.03)(0.995) = 0.00495 + 0.02985 = 0.0348. P(Leak|Alarm) = 0.00495/0.0348 ≈ 0.142 (≈14.2%) — most alarms are false, which is why alarms usually trigger a manual check rather than an automatic shutdown.
Exercise 1.5

A component is sourced from three suppliers, which partition every part received: Supplier X (50% of parts, 2% defect rate), Supplier Y (30% of parts, 4% defect rate), Supplier Z (20% of parts, 5% defect rate). Using the law of total probability, find the overall probability a randomly selected part is defective.

\(P(D) = P(D\mid X)P(X) + P(D\mid Y)P(Y) + P(D\mid Z)P(Z)\) = (0.02)(0.50) + (0.04)(0.30) + (0.05)(0.20) = 0.010 + 0.012 + 0.010 = 0.032 (≈3.2%).

§2

Random Variables

Turning uncertain outcomes into numbers you can average, weight, and design around.

Work Order #4488IoT Gateway · Packet Loss Log

Scenario. A factory-floor IoT gateway logs the number of dropped packets per minute (a whole-number count) and, separately, the time until a backup capacitor fails on the gateway board (any positive real number of hours).

  1. Which of these two measurements is discrete, and which is continuous — and why does that distinction change how you'd model each one?
  2. Given a week of dropped-packet counts, how do you summarize the "typical" number of drops per minute and how spread out that count tends to be?
  3. If each dropped packet costs the plant $0.40 in re-transmission overhead, what's the expected daily cost?
  4. Retry attempts are logged alongside dropped packets each minute (also 0/1). Do dropped packets and retries tend to move together, and how strongly?
Known values — one week of dropped-packet logs (PMF) P(X=0) = 0.55 · P(X=1) = 0.25 · P(X=2) = 0.13 · P(X=3) = 0.07 Re-transmission cost = $0.40/packet · 1440 minutes/day Joint PMF of X=drop indicator (0/1) and Y=retry indicator (0/1): P(0,0)=0.50, P(0,1)=0.05, P(1,0)=0.10, P(1,1)=0.35

Core ideas

Discrete random variable
Takes countable values (0, 1, 2, …). Described by a probability mass function (PMF): P(X = x).
Continuous random variable
Takes any value in an interval (e.g., time, length, voltage). Described by a probability density function (PDF); probabilities come from areas under the curve, not point values.
Expected value
The long-run average value of X, weighted by probability.
Variance & standard deviation
How far values typically spread from the expected value — critical for tolerance and risk decisions.
Covariance
Measures whether two random variables tend to move together (positive), move oppositely (negative), or show no linear relationship (near zero).
Correlation coefficient (ρ)
Covariance rescaled to always fall between −1 and +1, so its strength is comparable across variables measured in different units — the engineering-friendly version of "how related are these two things."
Expected value\(\displaystyle E[X] = \sum x \cdot P(X=x)\)
Variance\(\displaystyle \mathrm{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2\)
Linear transform\(\displaystyle E[aX+b] = a\,E[X] + b\)
Covariance\(\displaystyle \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = E[XY] - E[X]\,E[Y]\)
Correlation\(\displaystyle \rho = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X \cdot \sigma_Y}\)

Worked example

Bench notes — dropped packets per minute
Using the PMF from the work order:
Step 1 — expected value:
\(E[X] = 0(.55)+1(.25)+2(.13)+3(.07) = 0.72\) packets/min
Step 2 — \(E[X^2]\):
\(E[X^2] = 0(.55)+1(.25)+4(.13)+9(.07) = 1.40\)
Step 3 — variance & SD:
\(\mathrm{Var}(X) = 1.40 - 0.72^2 = 1.40 - 0.5184 = 0.8816 \Rightarrow SD \approx 0.939\)
Step 4 — expected daily cost (1440 min/day, $0.40/packet):
\(E[\text{cost}] = 1440 \times 0.72 \times \$0.40 \approx \$414.72\)/day
Answers → packet count is discrete (PMF), capacitor lifetime is continuous (PDF); expected drops ≈ 0.72/min with SD ≈ 0.94; expected daily re-transmission cost ≈ $415.
Bench notes — correlation between drops and retries
Using the joint PMF from the work order (X=drop indicator, Y=retry indicator):
Step 1 — marginal means:
\(E[X] = P(X=1) = 0.10+0.35 = 0.45, \quad E[Y] = P(Y=1) = 0.05+0.35 = 0.40\)
Step 2 — E[XY] (only the X=1,Y=1 cell contributes):
\(E[XY] = (1)(1)(0.35) = 0.35\)
Step 3 — covariance:
\(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = 0.35 - (0.45)(0.40) = 0.35 - 0.18 = 0.17\)
Step 4 — standard deviations (0/1 variables, so \(E[X^2]=E[X]\), \(E[Y^2]=E[Y]\)):
\(\mathrm{Var}(X) = 0.45-0.45^2 = 0.2475 \Rightarrow \sigma_X\approx0.4975, \quad \mathrm{Var}(Y) = 0.40-0.40^2 = 0.24 \Rightarrow \sigma_Y\approx0.4899\)
Step 5 — correlation:
\(\displaystyle \rho = \frac{0.17}{0.4975\times0.4899} \approx 0.698\)
Answer → ρ ≈ 0.70: drops and retries have a fairly strong positive correlation, meaning minutes with more dropped packets tend to also log more retry attempts — consistent with a shared root cause (e.g., a congested link) rather than two unrelated events.

§3

Distributions

Binomial, Poisson, Normal, Exponential — matching the model to the physical process.

Work Order #4502Plant-Wide Process Review

Scenario. Four different teams bring four different problems to the same weekly meeting:

  1. Assembly: "Out of every 20 solder joints, how many will be bad if each has a 2% independent failure chance?"
  2. IT: "Our server logs about 4 timeout events per hour on average — what's the chance we get more than 6 in a given hour?"
  3. Component eng.: "Our 10 kΩ resistors are rated ±5%. What fraction actually fall outside spec given the manufacturing spread?"
  4. Maintenance: "A pump fails on average every 500 hours. What's the chance it survives past 800 hours without failing?"

Each question calls for a different distribution — matching the right model to the physical process is half the job.

Known values Assembly: n = 20 joints, p = 0.02 failure probability each IT: server timeouts average λ = 4 events/hour Component eng.: resistors ~ Normal(μ = 10,000 Ω, σ = 250 Ω); spec limits 9,500–10,500 Ω Maintenance: pump fails on average every 500 hours (λ = 1/500 per hour)

Core ideas

Binomial(n, p)
Counts successes in n independent fixed trials, each with success probability p. Use for: fixed batch size, pass/fail per unit. Mean = np, Var = np(1−p).
Poisson(λ)
Counts events in a fixed window of time/space when events happen independently at a constant average rate λ. Use for: arrivals, timeouts, defects per unit area. Mean = Var = λ.
Normal(μ, σ)
Bell-shaped, symmetric — the default model for measurement error and manufacturing variation around a target. Convert to a z-score to use standard tables: z = (x − μ)/σ.
Exponential(λ)
Models time between independent events at a constant rate — classic "time to failure" model, memoryless (no wear-in accounted for).
Binomial\(\displaystyle P(X=k) = \binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\)
Poisson\(\displaystyle P(X=k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}\)
Exponential\(\displaystyle P(X>t) = e^{-\lambda t}\)

Worked example

Bench notes — resistor tolerance (Normal)
Using the component eng. values from the work order:
Step 1 — standardize the lower limit:
\(\displaystyle z = \frac{9500 - 10000}{250} = -2.00\)
Step 2 — standardize the upper limit:
\(\displaystyle z = \frac{10500 - 10000}{250} = +2.00\)
Step 3 — look up standard normal table:
\(P(Z < -2.00) \approx 0.0228, \quad P(Z > 2.00) \approx 0.0228\)
Step 4 — combine (out-of-spec on either side):
\(P(\text{out of spec}) = 0.0228 + 0.0228 = 0.0456\)
Answer → about 4.6% of resistors fall outside the ±5% tolerance band, even though the process is centered correctly — the spread (σ) itself is the problem, not the mean.

\(P(X=0) = e^{-4}(4^0)/0! = e^{-4} \approx\) 0.0183 (≈1.8%) — a clean hour is rare at this rate.

Data Visualization

Seeing the shape of your data before you trust any number that summarizes it.

Work Order #4519Motor Test Bench · Vibration Study

Scenario. You've pulled 300 vibration-amplitude readings (mm/s) from a motor test bench, plus torque values from three different bolt suppliers, plus an hour of continuous temperature readings from a bearing housing.

  1. Before running any test, how do you check whether the vibration readings are roughly symmetric or skewed, and whether there are any obvious outliers?
  2. How do you compare the torque distributions of three suppliers side-by-side in one view?
  3. How do you check whether bearing temperature is drifting upward over the test hour, versus just noisy?
Known values — bolt torque by supplier (N·m), target 40 N·m Supplier A: median 41, Q1 = 39, Q3 = 43, whiskers 35–47, no outliers Supplier B: median 41, Q1 = 38, Q3 = 44, whiskers 32–50, one outlier at 58 Supplier C: median 36, Q1 = 34, Q3 = 38, whiskers 30–42, no outliers

Core ideas

Histogram
Bins one numeric variable to reveal its shape — symmetric, skewed, bimodal — and roughly where the bulk of values sit.
Boxplot
Summarizes median, quartiles (Q1, Q3), and outliers in a compact strip — ideal for comparing several groups side by side.
Scatter plot
Shows the relationship between two numeric variables — direction, strength, linearity — and is the natural lead-in to regression (§7).
Run / time-series chart
Plots a measurement in the order it was collected, so you can catch trends, cycles, or shifts over time that a histogram would hide entirely.
IQR\(\displaystyle IQR = Q_3 - Q_1\)
Outlier flag\(\displaystyle x < Q_1 - 1.5\cdot IQR \ \text{ or } \ x > Q_3 + 1.5\cdot IQR\)

Worked example

Bench notes — reading a boxplot of bolt torque by supplier
Using the supplier values from the work order:
Step 1 — compute IQR per supplier:
\(A:\ IQR = 43-39 = 4 \quad B:\ IQR = 44-38 = 6 \quad C:\ IQR = 38-34 = 4\)
Step 2 — interpret: A and B share the same center (median 41), but B is noticeably more spread out and has an outlier — a consistency problem, not an accuracy problem. C is tightly consistent but centered lower, off the 40 N·m target — an accuracy problem, not a consistency one.
Takeaway → the boxplot separates "is it centered right" from "is it consistent," which a single average torque number would hide completely.

Statistical Inference

From a sample to a defensible claim — z-tests, t-tests, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests.

Work Order #4533Process & Materials Verification

Scenario. Two verification tasks land on your desk the same day. Part A: a machine's historical defect rate is well-established at 4% from years of production data, and a filling line's fill volume has a long-run known process standard deviation — in both cases the "true" spread is already known, which calls for a z-test. Part B: a new aluminum alloy batch needs to qualify against a minimum mean tensile strength of 310 MPa, but this time you only have a small sample (n=25) and the population standard deviation isn't known — it has to be estimated from the sample itself, which calls for a t-test instead.

  1. (Z) Using a z-test, is this month's defective proportion still consistent with the historical 4% rate?
  2. (Z) Using a z-test for the mean (σ known), is the average fill volume significantly different from the 500 mL target?
  3. (T) What range of values is the true population mean tensile strength of the new alloy plausibly in, at 95% confidence?
  4. (T) Is there statistically significant evidence the true mean tensile strength exceeds the 310 MPa minimum?
  5. If you approve the alloy batch and you're wrong, what does that Type I error cost the business — and what does a Type II error cost instead?
Known values Z-test, proportion: historical \(p_{0}\) = 0.04, this month n = 100, defective x = 9 (p̂ = 0.09) Z-test, mean: known process σ = 2.5 mL, n = 50, x̄ = 498.4 mL, target \(μ_{0}\) = 500 mL T-test: n = 25 coupons, x̄ = 314.2 MPa, s = 9.8 MPa \(H_0\): μ = 310 · \(H_1\): μ > 310 · α = 0.05

Core ideas

Point estimate & sampling variability
x̄ estimates μ, but every new sample gives a slightly different x̄ — that's why we report a range, not just a single number.
When to use a z-test vs. a t-test
Use a z-test when the population standard deviation σ is genuinely known (e.g., from years of stable process-control data), or the sample is large enough that s is a reliable stand-in. Use a t-test for the more common case where σ is unknown and has to be estimated from a small sample — that uncertainty is exactly why the t-distribution has heavier tails than the normal.
One-sample z-test for a mean
Tests whether a sample mean is consistent with a target \(μ_{0}\), using the known σ.
One-sample z-test for a proportion
Tests whether an observed proportion p̂ is consistent with a hypothesized proportion \(p_{0}\).
Confidence interval (t-based)
A range built from the sample that's expected to capture the true population parameter a specified percentage of the time under repeated sampling — using \(t^*\) instead of \(z^*\) because σ is unknown.
Hypothesis test
\(H_0\) (null, "no effect / at the spec") vs. \(H_1\) (alternative, "there is an effect / above spec"). A small p-value means the observed data would be unlikely if \(H_0\) were true.
Type I / Type II error
Type I: rejecting \(H_0\) when it's actually true (false alarm). Type II: failing to reject \(H_0\) when it's actually false (missed detection). Engineering context decides which error is more costly.
Z-test, mean\(\displaystyle z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\)
Z-test, proportion\(\displaystyle z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}\)
Two-sided critical value (z)\(z^{*} \approx \pm 1.96\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\)
Confidence interval (t)\(\displaystyle \bar{x} \pm t^{*}\cdot\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\)
Test statistic (t)\(\displaystyle t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}\)

Worked example

Bench notes — Part A: z-test verification
Proportion z-test, using the known values from the work order:
\(SE = \sqrt{0.04\times0.96/100} = \sqrt{0.000384} \approx 0.0196\)
\(\displaystyle z = \frac{0.09 - 0.04}{0.0196} \approx 2.55\)
\(|2.55| > 1.96 \Rightarrow\) reject \(H_0\) — defect rate has significantly increased from 4%
Mean z-test, using the known values:
\(\displaystyle SE = \frac{2.5}{\sqrt{50}} \approx 0.3536\)
\(\displaystyle z = \frac{498.4 - 500}{0.3536} \approx -4.53\)
\(|-4.53| > 1.96 \Rightarrow\) reject \(H_0\) — fill volume is significantly below the 500 mL target
Answers → both z-tests reject their null hypotheses: the defect rate has genuinely risen above the historical 4% baseline, and the fill line is running significantly under-target — both worth flagging for corrective action rather than dismissing as normal noise.
Bench notes — Part B: t-test qualification of the alloy batch
Using the known values from the work order:
Step 1 — standard error:
\(\displaystyle SE = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{9.8}{\sqrt{25}} = \frac{9.8}{5} = 1.96\)
Step 2 — 95% CI (\(t^{*} \approx 2.064\) for df=24):
\(314.2 \pm 2.064(1.96) = 314.2 \pm 4.05 \Rightarrow (310.15,\ 318.25)\) MPa
Step 3 — one-sided t-test statistic:
\(\displaystyle t = \frac{314.2 - 310}{1.96} = \frac{4.2}{1.96} \approx 2.14\)
Step 4 — compare to critical value (\(t_{0.05,24} \approx 1.711\)):
\(2.14 > 1.711 \Rightarrow\) reject \(H_0\)
Answers → 95% CI is (310.15, 318.25) MPa, which is entirely above 310; t=2.14 exceeds the critical value, so there's significant evidence the true mean exceeds spec. A wrongful Type I approval risks a structural failure downstream; a Type II rejection just wastes a good batch — in structural work, Type I is almost always the costlier error, which argues for a conservative α.

One-Way ANOVA

Comparing more than two group means at once, without stacking up t-test errors.

Work Order #4547Civil Lab · Concrete Curing Study

Scenario. Three concrete curing methods are being compared for 28-day compressive strength (MPa): Method A (water curing), Method B (membrane curing), Method C (steam curing). Five test cylinders per method.

  1. Why not just run three separate two-sample t-tests (A vs B, A vs C, B vs C) instead of one combined test?
  2. Is there a statistically significant difference among the three methods' mean strengths at all?
  3. If ANOVA says "yes, there's a difference somewhere," how do you find out which method(s) actually differ?
Known values k = 3 groups, n = 5 cylinders each, N = 15 total Group means: A = 34.2, B = 36.8, C = 39.1 MPa · Grand mean = 36.7 MPa From the raw cylinder data: SSB = 62.4, SSW = 48.0

Core ideas

Why not multiple t-tests
Each t-test carries its own 5% false-alarm risk; running three of them compounds the overall chance of at least one false "significant" result well above 5%. ANOVA tests all groups in one shot, controlling that overall error rate.
Between-group vs. within-group variation
ANOVA compares how much group means differ from each other (between) against how much individual values vary within each group (within, i.e. noise).
F-statistic
F = (between-group variance) / (within-group variance). A large F means group differences are large relative to natural noise.
Post-hoc tests
A significant F only tells you a difference exists somewhere. Pairwise post-hoc comparisons (e.g., Tukey's HSD) pinpoint which specific groups differ.
F-statistic\(\displaystyle F = \frac{MSB}{MSW} = \frac{SSB/(k-1)}{SSW/(N-k)}\)

Worked example

Bench notes — curing method ANOVA (summary stats)
Using the known values from the work order:
Step 1 — degrees of freedom:
\(df(\text{between}) = k-1 = 2, \quad df(\text{within}) = N-k = 12\)
Step 2 — mean squares:
\(\displaystyle MSB = \frac{62.4}{2} = 31.2, \quad MSW = \frac{48.0}{12} = 4.0\)
Step 3 — F statistic:
\(\displaystyle F = \frac{31.2}{4.0} = 7.80\)
Step 4 — compare to F-critical (\(F_{0.05,\,2,12} \approx 3.89\)):
\(7.80 > 3.89 \Rightarrow\) reject \(H_0\) (equal means)
Answers → single ANOVA avoids inflating false-alarm risk from 5% across three separate tests; F=7.80 exceeds critical value, so at least one curing method differs significantly; a Tukey post-hoc test would then be run to identify which pair(s) — likely steam (C) vs water (A), the two furthest apart.

Either works and gives an equivalent result for exactly two groups — but the two-sample t-test is the simpler, more direct tool. ANOVA's advantage (controlling error rate across many comparisons) only becomes necessary with three or more groups.

Regression

Fitting a line to predict one measurement from another — and knowing when to trust it.

Work Order #4561Facilities · CNC Energy Study

Scenario. Facilities wants to predict a CNC machine's daily energy use (kWh) from its daily runtime (hours), to help plan the electrical load. Five days of paired data were logged: (2,18), (4,31), (5,38), (7,52), (9,63) as (hours, kWh).

  1. What's the best-fit straight-line relationship between runtime and energy use?
  2. For every additional hour of runtime, how much extra energy should we expect?
  3. How much of the variation in energy use does runtime actually explain — and is it safe to predict energy use for a 14-hour day using this line?
Known values — (runtime hours, energy kWh) (2, 18) · (4, 31) · (5, 38) · (7, 52) · (9, 63)

Core ideas

Simple linear regression
Fits a line \(\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1x\) that minimizes the sum of squared residuals (least squares) — the vertical gaps between observed and predicted y.
Slope (\(b_{1}\))
The predicted change in y for each one-unit increase in x. Its sign and size are usually the actual engineering answer you need.
\(R^{2}\) (coefficient of determination)
The proportion of variation in y explained by the linear relationship with x, from 0 (no fit) to 1 (perfect fit).
Residuals
Observed minus predicted (y − ŷ). Patterns in residual plots (curves, funnels) signal that a straight line is the wrong model.
Extrapolation caution
A fitted line is only trustworthy within the range of x-values actually observed — predicting far outside that range assumes the relationship stays linear, which is often false.
Slope\(\displaystyle b_1 = \frac{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2}\)
Intercept\(\displaystyle b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1\bar{x}\)
R²\(\displaystyle R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{\text{residual}}}{SS_{\text{total}}}\)

Worked example

Bench notes — CNC runtime vs. energy use
Using the paired data from the work order:
Step 1 — means: \(\bar{x} = 5.4,\ \bar{y} = 40.4\)
Step 2 — slope numerator \(\sum(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})\):
\((-3.4)(-22.4)+(-1.4)(-9.4)+(-0.4)(-2.4)+(1.6)(11.6)+(3.6)(22.6)\)
\(= 76.16 + 13.16 + 0.96 + 18.56 + 81.36 = 190.2\)
Step 3 — slope denominator \(\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2\):
\(11.56+1.96+0.16+2.56+12.96 = 29.2\)
Step 4 — slope and intercept:
\(\displaystyle b_1 = \frac{190.2}{29.2} \approx 6.51, \quad b_0 = 40.4 - 6.51(5.4) \approx 5.25\)
Step 5 — fitted line and \(R^2\) (computed):
\(\hat{y} = 5.25 + 6.51x, \quad R^2 \approx 0.996\)
Answers → each extra hour of runtime costs ≈6.5 additional kWh; the line explains ~99.6% of the variation seen — a very strong fit within 2–9 hours; predicting a 14-hour day would be extrapolation (beyond the observed 2–9 hour range) and should be flagged as less reliable, even though the fit looks excellent inside that range.

Quality Control Charts

Watching a process live — telling ordinary noise apart from a real problem, in real time.

Work Order #4578Machine Shop · CNC Lathe Monitoring

Scenario. A CNC lathe cuts a shaft with a target diameter of 25.00 mm. Every hour, you pull a subgroup of n = 5 shafts and measure their diameters. After 20 subgroups: grand mean of subgroup means X̿ = 25.01 mm, mean subgroup range R̄ = 0.18 mm.

  1. What control limits should trigger an operator to stop and investigate the process, versus limits so tight they'd cause constant false alarms from ordinary noise?
  2. Subgroup 14 comes in with a mean of 25.24 mm — is that a real process problem, or expected variation?
  3. What's the difference between a "common cause" of variation (the machine's normal chatter) and a "special cause" (something actually went wrong)?
Known values Subgroup size n = 5, 20 subgroups: X̿ = 25.01 mm, R̄ = 0.18 mm Control-chart constants for n = 5: \(A_{2}\) = 0.577, \(D_{3}\) = 0, \(D_{4}\) = 2.114 Subgroup 14 mean = 25.24 mm

Core ideas

X̄ chart (X-bar chart)
Tracks the subgroup means over time — catches shifts in the process center.
R chart
Tracks the subgroup ranges over time — catches changes in process spread/consistency, and must be checked (and be in control) before the X̄ chart is trusted.
Center line, UCL, LCL
The center line is the process average; the upper/lower control limits are typically set at ±3 standard deviations of the subgroup statistic — the natural boundary of common-cause variation, not a customer spec limit.
Common cause vs. special cause
Common cause: inherent, expected noise (tool vibration, minor material variation) — the process is "in control." Special cause: an identifiable, fixable event (tool wear, a bad batch of stock, an operator change) that pushes a point outside the control limits or produces a non-random pattern.
Control limits vs. spec limits
Control limits come from the process itself and describe what it's actually doing; specification limits come from the customer/design and describe what's acceptable. A process can be perfectly "in control" and still be producing out-of-spec parts if it isn't capable enough — that's a separate question (process capability).
X̄ chart limits\(\displaystyle UCL = \bar{\bar{X}} + A_2\bar{R}, \quad LCL = \bar{\bar{X}} - A_2\bar{R}\)
R chart limits\(\displaystyle UCL = D_4\bar{R}, \quad LCL = D_3\bar{R}\)
Constants\(A_2, D_3, D_4\) depend on subgroup size \(n\)

Worked example

Bench notes — X̄ and R chart limits, n = 5
Using the known values from the work order:
Step 1 — X̄ chart control limits:
\(UCL(\bar{X}) = 25.01 + 0.577(0.18) = 25.01 + 0.1039 \approx 25.114\) mm
\(LCL(\bar{X}) = 25.01 - 0.1039 \approx 24.906\) mm
Step 2 — R chart control limits:
\(UCL(R) = 2.114(0.18) \approx 0.381\) mm
\(LCL(R) = 0(0.18) = 0\) mm
Step 3 — evaluate subgroup 14 (mean 25.24 mm):
\(25.24 > UCL(\bar{X}) = 25.114 \Rightarrow\) point falls outside the control limit
Answers → X̄ control limits are (24.906, 25.114) mm; subgroup 14's mean of 25.24 mm falls above UCL, signaling a likely special cause (investigate tool wear, offset drift, fixture slip) rather than ordinary noise; common-cause variation is the small, everyday scatter you'd see even on a healthy day, while a point beyond ±3σ (or a run of 7+ points trending one direction) flags something worth stopping the line for.

Reliability

How long systems survive — series vs. redundant designs, and the math of MTBF.

Work Order #4592Data Center · Power Subsystem Reliability

Scenario. A server rack's control unit depends on four components that must all work — power regulator, controller chip, cooling fan, network module — wired in series. Separately, the rack's primary power supply has a constant failure rate and must run continuously between planned maintenance windows. Engineers are also weighing whether to add a redundant backup power supply in parallel.

  1. What's the reliability of the 4-component series control unit?
  2. Using the exponential failure model, what's the probability the primary power supply survives to the next maintenance window, and what's its MTBF?
  3. If a redundant backup supply is added in parallel, how much does system reliability improve?
Known values Series control unit: R(regulator)=0.995, R(controller)=0.998, R(fan)=0.99, R(network)=0.997 Primary power supply: constant failure rate λ = 0.0002/hour; mission time t = 4000 hours Candidate redundant backup supply: R(4000 hrs) = 0.98

Core ideas

Reliability function R(t)
The probability a component or system survives past time t without failure: R(t) = P(T > t).
Hazard rate & the bathtub curve
The instantaneous failure rate over time typically follows a "bathtub" shape: high and falling early (infant mortality / defects), low and roughly constant in the middle (useful life), then rising again (wear-out).
MTBF / MTTF
Mean time between/to failure — the average lifetime. For a constant-hazard (exponential) process, MTBF = 1/λ.
Series systems
Every component must work for the system to work — system reliability is the product of individual reliabilities, so it's always lower than the weakest link.
Parallel (redundant) systems
The system works as long as at least one component works — redundancy raises reliability, often dramatically, especially for individually weak components.
Exponential reliability\(\displaystyle R(t) = e^{-\lambda t}\)
MTBF\(\displaystyle MTBF = \frac{1}{\lambda}\)
Series system\(\displaystyle R = R_1\cdot R_2 \cdots R_n\)
Parallel system\(\displaystyle R = 1-(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\cdots(1-R_n)\)

Worked example

Bench notes — resolving Work Order #4592
Using the known values from the work order:
Step 1 — series reliability of the control unit:
\(R = 0.995 \times 0.998 \times 0.99 \times 0.997 \approx 0.9803\)
Step 2 — exponential reliability of the primary power supply at t=4000h:
\(R(4000) = e^{-0.0002 \times 4000} = e^{-0.8} \approx 0.4493\)
Step 3 — MTBF:
\(\displaystyle MTBF = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{0.0002} = 5000\) hours
Step 4 — add the redundant backup supply in parallel:
\(R_{\text{parallel}} = 1 - (1-0.4493)(1-0.98) = 1 - (0.5507)(0.02) \approx 0.9890\)
Answers → the 4-component series unit is ≈98.0% reliable; the primary power supply alone has only ≈44.9% chance of reaching 4000 hours (MTBF = 5000 hours, so 4000 hours is a substantial fraction of its average life); adding the redundant backup lifts system reliability at 4000 hours to ≈98.9% — redundancy compensates heavily for a component whose reliability drops fast as mission time approaches its MTBF.

Study Designs

Observational vs. experimental — when your data can support a causal claim, and when it can't.

Work Order #4605Reliability Engineering · Lubricant Additive Study

Scenario. Plant leadership wants to know whether a new lubricant additive reduces bearing wear. Two proposals are on the table. Proposal 1 (Observational): pull two years of maintenance logs — some operators already started using the additive on their own — and compare wear rates between additive-users and non-users. Proposal 2 (Experimental): randomly assign identical machines to receive either the additive or the standard lubricant, then measure wear after a controlled trial period.

  1. Which design lets you make a causal claim — "the additive reduces wear" — rather than just an association?
  2. In the observational study, what's a plausible confounding variable that could make the additive look effective (or ineffective) even if it isn't?
  3. Why does random assignment in the experimental design help rule out that confounder?
Known values Proposal 1 (observational): 2 years of maintenance logs; additive use was self-selected by operators, not assigned Proposal 2 (experimental): n = 40 identical machines, randomly assigned 20/20 to additive vs. standard lubricant, 6-month controlled trial

Core ideas

Observational study
The researcher measures variables as they naturally occur, without intervening. Can reveal an association, but not — by itself — causation.
Experimental study
The researcher actively manipulates a factor (the treatment) and, ideally, randomly assigns units to groups. This is what supports a causal conclusion.
Confounding variable
A lurking variable related to both the exposure/treatment and the outcome, capable of creating a misleading association even when no real cause-effect link exists.
Randomization
Random assignment to groups balances both known and unknown confounders across groups on average, so any leftover difference in outcomes is more credibly attributable to the treatment itself.
Association ≠ causation
A strong observed relationship between two variables never, by itself, proves one causes the other — it may be coincidence, reverse causation, or a shared confounder.
Observationalshows association only — no causal claim
Randomized experimentassociation + control of confounders → causal claim

Worked example

Bench notes — resolving Work Order #4605
Using the two proposals from the work order:
Step 1 — classify Proposal 1:
No manipulation, no random assignment — additive use was self-selected by operators → observational study
Step 2 — identify a plausible confounder in Proposal 1:
Operators who proactively adopt a new additive may also be more diligent overall (better cleaning, alignment checks, lubrication schedule) — that diligence, not the additive itself, could explain lower wear
Step 3 — classify Proposal 2:
Active manipulation (assigns the treatment) plus random assignment of machines → experimental (randomized) study
Step 4 — why randomization addresses the confounder:
With random assignment, diligently-maintained and less-diligently-maintained machines end up split roughly evenly between the two groups — so "operator diligence" is balanced out and can no longer explain a difference in wear between groups
Answers → only Proposal 2 supports a causal claim; Proposal 1 can only establish that additive use and lower wear are associated, since operator diligence (and other unmeasured factors) remain plausible confounders that random assignment specifically neutralizes.

Chi-Square Tests

Testing categorical data — does the mix match expectations, and are two categories related?

Work Order #4618Quality Lab · Defect Pattern Analysis

Scenario. The quality team tracks defects by category (solder bridge, missed component, cold joint, misalignment) against a long-standing baseline mix. Separately, they suspect defect rates might differ between Day and Night shift and want to know if defect occurrence is actually related to shift, or just noise.

  1. Does this month's defect-category mix still match the historical baseline proportions, or has something shifted?
  2. Is whether a board is defective independent of which shift produced it, or is there a real association?
Known values Baseline proportions: solder bridge 40%, missed component 25%, cold joint 20%, misalignment 15% This month, n=200 total defects: solder bridge 92, missed component 40, cold joint 44, misalignment 24 Shift table: Day — 18 defective, 132 not, of 150 boards. Night — 30 defective, 120 not, of 150 boards

Core ideas

Chi-square goodness-of-fit test
Compares observed counts across categories to the counts expected under a hypothesized set of proportions — did the mix change?
Chi-square test of independence
Uses a contingency table of two categorical variables to test whether they're associated, or independent of each other.
Expected counts
Goodness-of-fit: expected = hypothesized proportion × n. Independence: expected = (row total × column total) / grand total.
Degrees of freedom
Goodness-of-fit: k−1 (k = number of categories). Independence: (rows−1)(columns−1).
Chi-square statistic\(\displaystyle \chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O-E)^2}{E}\)
df, goodness-of-fit\(\displaystyle df = k-1\)
df, independence\(\displaystyle df = (r-1)(c-1)\)

Worked example

Bench notes — resolving Work Order #4618
Part A — goodness-of-fit (defect mix vs. baseline), using the known values:
Expected counts (proportion × 200): 80, 50, 40, 30
\(\displaystyle \chi^2 = \frac{(92-80)^2}{80} + \frac{(40-50)^2}{50} + \frac{(44-40)^2}{40} + \frac{(24-30)^2}{30}\)
\(= 1.80 + 2.00 + 0.40 + 1.20 = 5.40\)
\(df = 4-1 = 3, \ \chi^2_{0.05,3} \approx 7.815 \Rightarrow 5.40 < 7.815 \Rightarrow\) fail to reject \(H_0\)
Part B — independence (defect status vs. shift), using the known values:
Expected: Day-Def=24, Day-NotDef=126, Night-Def=24, Night-NotDef=126
\(\displaystyle \chi^2 = \frac{(18-24)^2}{24} + \frac{(132-126)^2}{126} + \frac{(30-24)^2}{24} + \frac{(120-126)^2}{126}\)
\(= 1.50 + 0.286 + 1.50 + 0.286 \approx 3.57\)
\(df = (2-1)(2-1) = 1, \ \chi^2_{0.05,1} \approx 3.841 \Rightarrow 3.57 < 3.841 \Rightarrow\) fail to reject \(H_0\)
Answers → this month's defect mix is not significantly different from baseline (\(\chi^2=5.40 < 7.815\)); defect status and shift also show no statistically significant association at α=0.05, though at 3.57 vs. a 3.841 threshold it's close enough to keep watching in future months rather than dismiss outright.

§12

A/B Testing

Comparing two variants head-to-head — and telling a real lift apart from noise.

Work Order #4646Firmware Team · Update Success Rate A/B Test

Scenario. The firmware team is rolling out a large sensor-network update and wants to know whether a new "chunked" update protocol improves success rate over the current "single-file" protocol. They run an A/B test: Group A (current protocol) and Group B (new chunked protocol), both deployed simultaneously to randomly selected nodes under identical network conditions.

  1. Is the difference in success rates between A and B statistically significant, or could it be due to chance?
  2. What's the practical impact — how many more nodes would succeed at scale (say, 10,000 nodes) if B is rolled out?
  3. What design choices here (randomization, running both variants at the same time, holding conditions constant) make this a valid comparison rather than just an observational one?
Known values Group A (control): \(n_{A}\) = 500 nodes, 430 successful updates (\(\hat{p}_A = 0.86\)) Group B (treatment): \(n_{B}\) = 500 nodes, 460 successful updates (\(\hat{p}_B = 0.92\))

Core ideas

A/B testing
A randomized controlled experiment (see §10) comparing two variants — control (A) and treatment (B) — on a single outcome metric, with units randomly assigned to one group or the other.
Two-sample z-test for proportions
Tests whether the two groups' observed success proportions differ by more than sampling noise would explain, using a pooled proportion under the assumption of no difference.
Statistical vs. practical significance
A statistically significant result just means the difference is unlikely to be chance — it doesn't guarantee the difference is big enough to be worth the engineering cost of rolling it out. Both need to be checked.
Why randomize and run concurrently
Random assignment plus running both variants at the same time under the same conditions rules out confounders like time-of-day network load or gradual infrastructure changes — the same logic from §10, applied to a live rollout.
Pooled proportion\(\displaystyle \hat{p} = \frac{x_A + x_B}{n_A + n_B}\)
Standard error\(\displaystyle SE = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_A} + \frac{1}{n_B}\right)}\)
Test statistic\(\displaystyle z = \frac{\hat{p}_B - \hat{p}_A}{SE}\)

Worked example

Bench notes — resolving Work Order #4646
Using the known values from the work order:
Step 1 — pooled proportion:
\(\displaystyle \hat{p} = \frac{430+460}{500+500} = \frac{890}{1000} = 0.89\)
Step 2 — standard error:
\(SE = \sqrt{0.89\times0.11\times(1/500+1/500)} = \sqrt{0.0979\times0.004} = \sqrt{0.0003916} \approx 0.0198\)
Step 3 — z statistic:
\(\displaystyle z = \frac{0.92 - 0.86}{0.0198} \approx 3.03\)
Step 4 — decision:
\(|3.03| > 1.96 \Rightarrow\) reject \(H_0\) — statistically significant improvement
Step 5 — practical impact at scale (10,000 nodes):
\(\text{Lift} = 0.92 - 0.86 = 0.06 \Rightarrow 0.06 \times 10{,}000 = 600\) additional successful updates
Answers → the improvement is statistically significant (z≈3.03); at scale it means roughly 600 fewer failed updates per 10,000 nodes — a practically meaningful reduction in manual-intervention cases; the result is trustworthy specifically because both protocols were randomly assigned and run at the same time under matched conditions, ruling out the kind of confounding discussed in §10.
